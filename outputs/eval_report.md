> **`q11_nf4_attributed_to_lora` scores 0.00 on every configuration, and that is the honest and expected result.** The question asks which section of *the LoRA paper* describes NF4 quantisation. NF4 belongs to QLoRA; LoRA contains no quantisation scheme at all. Because the question names the wrong paper, the query text itself steers both retrievers straight into LoRA, and the QLoRA chunks that would correct the premise never reach the top 5. No amount of retrieval tuning fixes this — the premise is wrong, so the best passage for the query as asked is not the passage that answers it.

This is a finding about the architecture, not a bug to tune away: **correcting a false premise is not a retrieval problem.** It requires the agent to notice that the retrieved evidence does not support what the question assumes, which is the sufficiency judge's job at Step 11 and the abstention path's job at Step 7 — both downstream of everything measured here. The retrieval numbers should be read as: retrieval finds the right passage for well-posed questions, and the false-premise case is deliberately handed to a later stage.

## 2 · Measured routing thresholds

Thresholds are read off the observed cross-encoder score distribution on this corpus. They are not carried over from a previous project: a different corpus produces a different distribution, and reusing a number across the two is how routing silently degrades.

**How the populations were built.** Two decisions need two populations. *Routing* looks at one number per question -- the top rerank score on the slate -- so its populations hold one score per question. *Verification* looks at one (sentence, chunk) pair at a time, so its populations hold one score per chunk. Deriving a routing threshold from the per-pair distribution is a category error: a question whose best gold chunk scores 0.99 routes to ANSWER regardless of whether its third gold chunk scores 0.18. Positives are the gold chunks of answerable questions. Negatives are every chunk retrieved for a *control* question — a question the corpus cannot answer at all. Taking "non-gold chunks retrieved for an answerable question" as the negative population instead would be badly contaminated, because a chunk that was not listed as gold is very often still relevant. The controls have no correct answer anywhere in the corpus, so every chunk retrieved for them is a genuine negative.

A point estimate drawn from this many positives would be a number with no error bars presented as though it had them. The curve shows how sharply the routing actually depends on the threshold.

Balanced-accuracy peak at **tau = 0.85** (93.8%).

| Threshold | Value | How it was derived | Used for |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `TAU_LOW` | 0.8 | populations OVERLAP (best control 0.829 >= weakest answerable 0.789), so no clean cut exists. Set 0.05 below the sweep's balanced-accuracy peak (0.85). | below this, refuse |
| `TAU_HIGH` | 0.99 | 0.15 above the sweep peak (0.85), widening the clarify band to cover the region where the two populations genuinely mix. | above this, answer directly |
| `TAU_VERIFY` | 0.378 | p95 of the per-chunk control distribution (n=100) — 95% of chunks retrieved for an unanswerable question score below this | groundedness floor for one cited sentence |

> **The two routing populations overlap.** No single threshold separates answerable questions from controls on this set, so the clarify band was widened around the sweep's peak rather than a clean cut being asserted. The overlap is itself a finding and is reported rather than smoothed over.

Between `TAU_LOW` and `TAU_HIGH` the agent asks a clarifying question rather than guessing.

> **Stated plainly: these thresholds are tuned, not learned, and the positive population is small.** They are derived from the same question set the routing is later reported on, so route accuracy measured against them is optimistic by construction. The sweep above is published so a reader can see how much the choice actually matters rather than taking the point estimate on trust.
